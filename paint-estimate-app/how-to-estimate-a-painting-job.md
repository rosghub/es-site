# How to Estimate a Painting Job, Room by Room

<ArticleByline />

<ArticleBanner

Most painting estimates go wrong in the house, not in the math. A ceiling that
never got measured, a closet nobody wrote down, a repair that turned into a day
of prep. This guide covers how our co-founder walks a job, what to record in
each room, and what to check before the estimate goes out.

## Walk the job twice

Our co-founder walks every job twice, and each pass has a different purpose.

### Pass one, with the client

Early on, he used the first walk to talk about his crew and his process. Most
clients didn't care. "They wanted to tell me about the last painter and what
they're nervous about this time," he says. Now he mostly listens. "You have two
ears and one mouth. Use them accordingly."

Use this pass to name the rooms, take photos, and note what the client cares
about: the stain they want gone, the drop cloths the last painter skipped, the
worry about paying a deposit. Those notes shape the estimate as much as the
measurements do.

### Pass two, alone

The second walk is for measuring and counting: length, width and height,
doors, windows, trim, and anything that needs repair. Without a conversation
going, it's quick, and you're less likely to skip the hallway.

## What to record in each room

His first estimates lived in a notebook: "client's name at the top, then the
room, rough dimensions, a tally of windows and doors, and every note I could
scribble." That list still holds up. For each room, write down:

- **A room name** like "Primary bedroom" or "Hall bath," so you and the crew
  mean the same room.
- **Length, width and height.** Rough is fine. You're estimating, not ordering
  drywall.
- **Doors and windows,** as a count, with a note on anything unusual.
- **Trim:** baseboard, crown, chair rail, casing.
- **Extras:** radiators, fireplaces, closets, built-ins, railings.
- **Repairs and prep:** cracks, nail pops, water stains, peeling paint.
- **Coats** for each surface, especially where the color is changing.
- **Photos and notes** for anything you'll need to remember later.

### The room math

Length, width and height give you the three numbers most interior rooms need:

- **Perimeter** = 2 × (length + width)
- **Wall area** = perimeter × height
- **Ceiling area** = length × width

A 23 by 10 foot room with 9-foot ceilings has a 66-foot perimeter, 594 square
feet of wall and 230 square feet of ceiling. The perimeter also prices linear
work like baseboard and chair rail.

Coats multiply both labor and paint, so record them per surface instead of

## Put what you're not doing in writing

Scope that isn't on the estimate tends to become scope you do for free. Three
habits help.

**List what you're skipping.** If the basement bath is about to be remodeled,
say so on the estimate instead of leaving it off. A line marked "Not Included"
tells the client you saw it and tells the crew to leave it alone. As our
co-founder puts it, "That's how you keep the crew from painting the powder
room."

**Price the favors.** He's candid about this one: "when I'm being nice to a
client, I give away work. 'Sure, we'll touch up that closet.'" A quick time and
materials line keeps the extra visible to the client, even if you discount it.

**Spell out the prep.** Moving furniture, masking, patching, sanding and
priming all take real time. "Spelling out the prep is what separates a
professional estimate from the cheaper bid," and it shows the client what
they're paying for.

## Check the estimate before you send it

One of the most common estimating mistakes isn't a bad rate. It's a missing
number: a room with no ceiling height, or a wall that never got measured. Our
co-founder has "seen other programs show a ceiling as 'included' with zero time
calculated."

Before you send, check that:

- every room has all of its dimensions
- every surface has the right number of coats
- setup, prep and repairs are on their own lines
- anything you're skipping is marked not included
- paint quantities are rounded up to whole gallons
- the client's name, email and address are filled in

## Send it while they still remember you

The best time to send an estimate is while the client still remembers the
walkthrough. When you price on a phone, "the estimate gets finished in the
house, not at the kitchen table at 11 p.m."

If you do write estimates at night, wait to send them. "Nobody wants one
landing in a client's inbox at 11:40 p.m." If you email from Gmail, use its
built-in Schedule send (the arrow next to Send) to hold it until morning.
That's a Gmail trick, not an EstimateSync feature.
