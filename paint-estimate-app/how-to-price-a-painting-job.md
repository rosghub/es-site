# How to Price a Painting Job: Square-Foot Rates vs. Pricing the Time

Ask how to price a painting job and someone will give you a square-foot number.
It's simple, and some experienced painters use it well. For a newer painter,
it's also one of the fastest ways to underbid.

## The trouble with square-foot pricing

Our co-founder sees new painters get burned by it. "You read 'charge a few
dollars a square foot and you'll do fine,' and then a picky client wants extra
prep and a few custom colors." You finish, work out the hours afterward, and
"realize you barely paid yourself for two weeks. It's gut-wrenching to see how
badly you underbid."

A square-foot rate hides what actually drives the job: prep, coats, color
changes, cutting in, ceiling height, furniture, and how particular the client
is. Two rooms with the same square footage can take very different amounts of
time.

His rule of thumb: "A flat square-foot rate only works once you have a lot of
your own job data behind it. Until then, price the time."

## How to price the time

Pricing the time means estimating the hours for each surface, then turning
those hours into a price.

1. **Measure each surface.** Area for walls and ceilings, length for trim, a
   count for doors and windows.
2. **Apply a production rate,** meaning how much of that surface one painter
   finishes in an hour. Labor hours = quantity × coats ÷ production rate.
3. **Add setup and prep as their own lines:** moving furniture, masking,
   patching, sanding, priming.
4. **Multiply hours by your labor rate.** It should cover wages, payroll taxes,
   insurance and overhead, not just what you pay the painter.
5. **Add materials.** Gallons = area × coats ÷ spread rate, rounded up, times
   your paint cost. Add sundries like tape, plastic and caulk.
6. **Add your markup** and check the margin before you send.

Production rates are personal. They depend on your crew, your tools and the
work you take on. Compare actual hours to estimated hours on a few jobs and
adjust your rates from what you find.

## Other ways to price, and when they fit

Most painters mix methods on the same job. Our co-founder adds up labor hours
on most of his jobs, but "everyone calculates the same kind of item
differently." One painter prices doors by the hour, another per door, another
as part of the room.

- **Flat price per room or item** works for work you've done so many times you
  know the number, like a standard bedroom or a front door. Keep an eye on the
  hours behind it.
- **Time and materials** fits repairs, extra prep, and anything you can't see
  until you open it up.
- **Square foot** works once your own job history tells you what a square foot
  really costs you.

## Markup is not margin

Markup is profit as a share of your cost. Margin is profit as a share of the
price. On a job that costs you $1,000, a 25% markup adds $250 and makes the
price $1,250. That $250 is 25% of the cost but only 20% of the price.

<MarkupMarginChart />

The same job always shows a smaller margin than markup, so a painter who thinks
in markup can believe the job pays better than it does. Check gross profit and
margin, not just the total, before the estimate goes out.
